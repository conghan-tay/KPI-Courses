# PART 1 — Manuscript draft, *Hold Your Number*, chapters 1–3 (unedited)

## Chapter 1: The objection you hear is never the objection you have

Here is the call I have had four hundred times. Somebody forwards me a lost deal. The email says "they went with someone cheaper." I ask one question: what did they compare you to? And about eight times in ten, the answer is a shrug. They don't know. They never asked.

That is not a pricing problem. That is a problem you had six weeks earlier and are only now finding out about.

I spent eleven years on the other side of this, selling pumps into plants. I sat in rooms where the price got argued over for ninety minutes. And I can tell you that in every single one of those rooms, the outcome had been settled before anybody opened their mouth about money. It was settled when the maintenance engineer wrote the spec. If the spec said "positive displacement, 60 GPM, ANSI flange," we were one of four vendors and the meeting was a formality about two percent. If the spec said our part number, the meeting was theater.

A price objection is almost never about the price. By the time somebody is arguing with your number, the real failure is upstream. Either you are talking to a person who cannot sign, or you never got them to say out loud what the problem is costing them. Those are the only two. I have looked for a third for seventeen years.

The reason this matters is that people respond to a price objection by doing price things. They discount. They add a tier. They redo the deck. All of that is treatment applied to the wrong organ.

## Chapter 2: Find the number before you find the solution

The first call is not for describing what you do. Nobody has ever bought anything because a vendor described what they do well.

The first call has exactly two jobs. Leave with a range, and leave with a name. The range is what they are prepared to spend, out of their mouth, not yours. The name is the person who signs. If you end a first call and you have neither, you did not have a sales call. You had a nice chat and you are now going to spend eleven hours writing a proposal for a stranger.

People tell me you can't ask about budget on a first call, it's rude. It is not rude. It is rude to take a buyer's Tuesday, then your own week, and then produce a number that was never in the realm. Procurement people ask about money in the first four minutes and nobody thinks they are rude. They think they are procurement people.

Here is how I ask it, and I have not changed the words in nine years:

> "Before I go build something — do you have a number in your head for this? I'm not going to hold you to it. I just don't want to send you something that's off by ten times in either direction."

The "either direction" is doing all the work. It gives them permission to say a big number without feeling like a mark.

Now — what if they won't say? Then you say a number first, and you make it a range, and you make the bottom of the range higher than you're comfortable with. "Work like this usually runs eighty to a hundred and forty. Does that end the conversation?" You are not negotiating. You are testing whether there is a conversation.

If the answer is "oh, we were thinking more like fifteen," you have just saved yourself a week. That is a good outcome. Losing in minute six is a business model. Losing in week three is a tax.

## Chapter 3: What the problem costs

Everybody in services has been told to sell value, not hours. Fine. Nobody tells you the mechanical part, which is that "value" is a number the *buyer* says, not a number you calculate in a spreadsheet and present.

You are trying to get one sentence out of them. It sounds like: "if this doesn't get fixed by Q3 we lose the renewal, which is about four hundred grand." That sentence is the entire deal. Once they have said it, your number is being compared to four hundred thousand instead of being compared to a freelancer on a marketplace.

If you cannot get that sentence, one of three things is true. They don't know the number, which means they haven't got approval and you're early. They know it and won't say it, which means they don't trust you yet. Or there isn't one, which means this is a nice-to-have and it will die in committee in March. All three of those are useful. None of them are solved by a better proposal.

The question I use is not "what's the impact." Everybody says impact and everybody gets adjectives back. I ask: **"What happens if you do nothing until next year?"** Doing nothing is a real option they have actually considered, so they have actually priced it. They'll tell you.

# PART 2 — Podcast transcript: *The Revenue Desk*, ep. 112 (partial)

**HOST:** ...so let's get into the thing you posted that got you a lot of angry replies. You said free discovery calls are a mistake.

**DANA:** I said something slightly narrower than that, but I'll defend the wide version too.

**HOST:** Go ahead.

**DANA:** Free discovery is a training program. It teaches the buyer that your thinking is worth zero and your doing is worth money. Then you spend the rest of the relationship confused about why they push back on strategy fees and never push back on headcount.

Whatever you give away for free is what the buyer decides the category is worth. If you hand over a two-hour diagnosis, a stakeholder map and a rough plan for nothing, you've told them the diagnosis is a sales cost. It isn't. It's the highest margin thing you will ever produce.

**HOST:** But everyone does free consultations. That's the norm.

**DANA:** Everyone also loses forty percent of their proposals to no-decision. The norm isn't doing great.

**HOST:** So what, you invoice for a first call?

**DANA:** No. Don't be silly. The first call is free and it's twenty-five minutes and its only job is the two things — the range and the name. What I charge for is the *assessment*. Same conversation you were going to have for free, except it has a name, a deliverable, a date and an invoice. Six to twelve grand, two weeks, ends in a document and a recommendation.

**HOST:** And people pay that.

**DANA:** People pay that, and here's the part nobody expects — they *behave* differently once they've paid. They show up to the sessions. They bring the finance guy. They send the data you asked for. A buyer who has paid you eight thousand dollars has told their boss about you. Now they need you to be good.

**HOST:** Hm.

**DANA:** The free version, you're a vendor being evaluated. The paid version, you're already working together and the big engagement is the obvious next step, and it isn't a proposal, it's a continuation.

**HOST:** Let me push on that. My audience is a lot of five-to-fifteen person shops. They'd say: nobody pays for a scoping call.

**DANA:** Nobody pays for a scoping call. Correct. They pay for an assessment with a deliverable and a date. It is the same call with a different name and an invoice attached. If you can't sell a paid assessment, you were never going to sell the hundred and forty thousand dollar engagement, you just hadn't found out yet.

**HOST:** Okay. Different topic. Proposals.

**DANA:** Proposals are where good deals go to die quietly.

**HOST:** Say more.

**DANA:** Never send a proposal that hasn't already been said out loud. That's the rule. Every number, every phase, every exclusion — the buyer should have heard it from your mouth, on a call, with a chance to react, before it exists as a PDF.

**HOST:** Why does that matter so much?

**DANA:** Because a document can't handle an objection. If your number surprises somebody in a PDF, you find out three days later by email, if you find out at all. Usually you find out by silence. On a call, when the number surprises them, you watch their face do something and you deal with it in the next four seconds. That's the whole job.

**HOST:** What about enterprise? They require written proposals.

**DANA:** Sure. Then the written proposal goes out *after* the call, and it's a record of what you agreed, not a pitch. Send it as confirmation. "Here's what we said." Nobody has ever been talked into anything by a PDF, and plenty of people have been talked out of things by one.

**HOST:** Do you have a version of that for people who feel awkward reading numbers out loud?

**DANA:** They feel awkward because they don't believe the number. That's a different fix and we should talk about it separately.

> Note: Tomás runs a productized design studio. He and Dana disagree on most of this episode. Recorded live.

**HOST:** Tomás, you publish all your pricing on your website. Dana, you don't. Fight.

**TOMÁS:** My position is simple: you should publish full pricing publicly, always, no exceptions. Hiding your prices is a tell. It says you're going to charge different people different amounts based on what you think they can afford, and buyers know it, and they resent it. Transparency is the whole reason our inbound works.

**DANA:** It works for you because you sell one thing in one shape. You've productized. Good for you, genuinely. But for anybody doing custom work, a published price is a ceiling with your own name on it. You've set the anchor before you know what the problem costs the buyer.

**TOMÁS:** Or you've filtered out the people who were never going to pay it.

**DANA:** You've filtered out the people who would have paid three times it, because they read fourteen thousand on a page and never called. I'd rather have the awkward conversation than lose the deal silently.

**HOST:** Tomás, you also said something before we started rolling about logo deals.

**TOMÁS:** Yeah — I think discounting hard for a marquee client is one of the best investments a young firm can make. Take the Nike deal at forty percent off. The logo pays you back for five years in credibility and referrals. I'd do it every time.

**DANA:** And this is where we're going to have a problem.

**TOMÁS:** I know.

**DANA:** Logos don't pay salaries. And the referral value everybody cites is unmeasurable at that size — nobody in this conversation has ever run the counterfactual. What is measurable is that you now have a marquee client who knows your real price is sixty percent of your stated price, they will renew at that number forever, and their VP will tell three peers what they paid.

**TOMÁS:** That hasn't been my experience.

**DANA:** It's been mine, twice, expensively. If you want the logo, give them the full price and take scope out of the box. Cut the number of deliverables, cut the hours of access, cut the reporting. Same rate, less work. Then the logo costs you delivery cost instead of costing you your price integrity.

**HOST:** That's your general discount rule, right?

**DANA:** Never lower the price without taking something out of the box. Not once, not for logos, not at quarter end. The moment a number moves because somebody asked, you've told them the number was never real. And they don't forget it — they retest it at renewal, and every negotiation after that starts from the discounted number, not the real one.

**TOMÁS:** I think that's rigid.

**DANA:** It is rigid. That's the feature.

**HOST:** Let's do tiers before we run out. Three-option proposals — good, bad?

**DANA:** Mostly bad, and I'm aware I'm in the minority. Three tiers is a discount nobody asked you for. The theory is that the middle looks reasonable next to the top one. What actually happens is that a buyer who was ready to pay the top number takes the middle, because you offered it and it's cheaper and nobody gets fired for the middle option.

**TOMÁS:** Our conversion rate went up when we added tiers.

**DANA:** Conversion to the middle. Look at average deal size instead of close rate. Every firm I've made run that number found the same thing, and about half of them stopped doing tiers that week.

**HOST:** So one option, take it or leave it?

**DANA:** One recommendation. If you want a second option, make it *more* expensive and more ambitious, not less. Then the conversation is about scope up, not scope down.

# PART 4 — Community AMA, March (raw paste, lightly cleaned)

**Q: Client says our number is too expensive compared to another agency. What do I say?**

Ask what they compared it to. Specifically — which firm, what scope, what number. Nine times in ten they can't answer, because "too expensive" is the polite way to end a conversation without giving a reason. If they *can* answer and the other scope is genuinely comparable, then you have a real competitor and a real conversation, and that's fine. But you should know which situation you're in before you say another word about money. Do not start defending a number against a competitor who might not exist.

**Q: How do I raise prices on existing clients without losing them?**

You give a date and you give a reason that isn't about you. "Effective July 1, this program is 12,500 a month." Not "we've grown a lot and our costs have gone up," which is a story about your problems and invites them to have opinions about your problems.

Sixty days' notice. In writing, but say it on a call first — same rule as proposals. Expect to lose one. Budget for it emotionally before you send it. The one you lose is almost always the one that was eating your team alive, and you know which one I mean.

**Q: We got invited to an RFP by a big company. Any tips?**

If you're finding out about the RFP when it's published, you're column fodder. Somebody shaped that document months ago and it's shaped like whoever shaped it. Answer it if you have spare capacity, but understand the odds and don't reorganize your quarter around it.

The real lesson is upstream: by the time procurement is in the room, the pricing was decided months ago. Whoever writes the requirements picks the comparison set. Your job is to be in the building when the requirements get written, which means talking to the person with the problem long before there's a budget line for it.

**Q: Do you ever go under your number?**

Yes. When I take work out of the box. Never because somebody asked.

**Q: What's your read on the "just charge more" advice that's everywhere?**

It's half right and it's useless as delivered. Charging more without changing who you talk to just moves your loss from month three to month one. The sequence is: change who you're in the room with, get the cost of the problem said out loud, then raise the number. In that order. People try to do step three first because it's the only one that fits in a tweet.

# PART 5 — Newsletter, January: "The hourly thing — I was wrong"

I've been telling people for years: never bill hourly, ever, under any circumstances. Hourly billing punishes you for getting faster and turns your buyer into an auditor of your timesheet.

I still believe most of that. But "never, ever" was wrong and I've been corrected enough times by people with better data than mine that I should say so in public.

The narrow case where hourly is correct: when the buyer's own system cannot issue a purchase order for a fixed fee. This is real and it's more common than I thought. Regulated industries, some government work, a lot of large-enterprise staff-augmentation budget lines. The money in that budget line is *literally* denominated in hours. If you insist on a fixed fee there, you are not being principled, you are being unpurchasable, and a competitor who fills in the form correctly gets the work.

So the rule I'd write now: bill for the outcome wherever the buyer's system permits it. Where it doesn't, take the hourly line, set the rate high enough that the timesheet argument is boring, and cap the engagement so it can't become a body-shop relationship by drift.

The thing I got wrong wasn't the principle. It was pretending that a principle is worth more than a signed contract.

# PART 6 — Workshop handout notes (unformatted, for the pricing workshop)

Effective hourly rate — how to actually compute it. Take the fee. Subtract direct delivery cost (contractors, tooling, travel). Divide by total hours logged including sales, revisions, and the meetings nobody logs. Most firms are shocked; the "premium" retainer is usually the worst line item once you count revision cycles.

Payment terms to use as defaults: 50% on signature, 50% at midpoint for projects under 12 weeks. Monthly in advance for retainers, never in arrears. Net 15. Late fee stated in the contract at 1.5% monthly even if you never charge it — its job is to move you up the AP queue, not to collect.

SOW structure that has never caused a fight: Outcome (one paragraph, what will be true when we're done) / In scope (bulleted, specific) / Explicitly out of scope (this section prevents more disputes than the rest of the document combined) / Assumptions and dependencies (what you need from them, by when, and what happens to the timeline if it's late) / Change process (how a new request becomes a new number, stated before anybody wants one) / Fee and schedule.

Change orders: write the process in the SOW while everyone is happy. A change request mid-project is not a negotiation if the mechanism was agreed in advance; it's just paperwork.

Discount authority: if you have salespeople, the answer is that they have none. Every exception routes to one person. Firms lose their pricing the same way — not in one bad decision, in forty small ones nobody logged.

Retainer sizing rule of thumb: monthly retainer should be at least 1/8 of what a comparable project engagement would cost, or the retainer is a discount for commitment you were going to get anyway.

Contract renewals — calendar them at 90 days out, not 30. At 30 days you're negotiating under a deadline and the deadline is yours, not theirs.
